> **No statement is immune to revision.**
>
> W. V. O. Quine, Two Dogmas of Empiricism (1951)

Much of human knowledge can be expressed in the form of rules. From observations of the world, we derive statements such as “if this holds, then that follows.” By applying multiple rules in sequence, we can infer conclusions that are not explicitly given.

However, different reasoning paths do not necessarily agree. Additional premises or alternative paths may support opposite conclusions about the same proposition. Thus, conflict is not merely an anomaly in rule-based reasoning, but can arise naturally from the interaction of individually valid rules.

The key question is therefore not simply whether a conflict exists, but **where it arises and whether it can be resolved by conditioning on its source**. This allows us to distinguish globally conflicting structures from cases in which a single premise itself produces incompatible conclusions.


> X is a melon. ($\ell_1$)  
>
> X is a watermelon. ($\ell_2$)  
>
> Watermelon is a melon. ($R_1: \ell_2 \rightarrow \ell_1$)    
>
> Melons are usually light green inside. ($R_2 : \ell_1 \rightarrow \ell_3$)    
>
> Watermelons are not green inside. ($\neg \ell_3$)  
>
> Thus, both polarities of the same atom are supported. ($\ell_3$ and $\neg \ell_3$)

When more rules and initial facts are given, we can consider the following reasoning paths:

We analyze reasoning relations between a **source atom** $a_s$ and a **target atom** $a_t$.


## 1. Polarity Transition Types

Consider a source atom $a_s$ and a target atom $a_t$.

A directed relation between their literals can take one of four polarity forms:

- $a_s \rightarrow a_t$: Positive to Positive

- $a_s \rightarrow \neg a_t$: Positive to Negative

- $\neg a_s \rightarrow a_t$: Negative to Positive

- $\neg a_s \rightarrow \neg a_t$: Negative to Negative

These describe the polarity of the source and target literals, independently of how many reasoning steps connect them.

When **classical contraposition**

$$
\ell_1 \rightarrow \ell_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\neg \ell_2 \rightarrow \neg \ell_1
$$

is additionally assumed, each rule may induce a contraposed rule in the reverse direction. This augments the rule set, while the four polarity-transition types above remain unchanged.

## 2. Types of Reasoning Structure

We distinguish the **structural topology of reasoning paths** from the **polarity status of their conclusions**.
A reasoning structure may contain a single path or multiple interacting paths, regardless of whether the resulting conclusions are consistent or conflicting.


### Linear Chain

A reasoning structure is a **linear chain** if it consists of a single directed sequence of literals

$$
(\ell_0, \ell_1, \ldots, \ell_k),
$$

where each consecutive pair is connected by a rule

$$
\ell_i \rightarrow \ell_{i+1}.
$$

The sequence represents a derivation from the source literal $\ell_0$ to the target literal $\ell_k$.

$$

### Endpoint Abstraction

A multi-hop reasoning process may induce a simpler relation between its source and target.

Consider a reasoning path

$$
\tau=(\ell_0,\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_k).
$$

Instead of retaining every intermediate step, we may consider its endpoint relation

$$
\ell_0 \leadsto \ell_k.
$$

However, such an abstraction is meaningful only when a source determines a **unique target polarity**.

For a source literal $s$ and target atom $a$, let the possible target literals reachable from $s$ be

$$
O_G(s,a)
=
\{
\ell \in \{a,\neg a\}
\mid
s \leadsto_G \ell
\}.
$$

The source-target relation $(s,a)$ is **endpoint-abstractable** if

$$
|O_G(s,a)|=1.
$$

That is, reasoning from $s$ determines exactly one polarity of $a$.

If instead

$$
|O_G(s,a)|=2,
$$

then both $a$ and $\neg a$ are reachable from the same source, and the relation is **non-abstractable**.


### Polarity-Consistent

A target atom may be reached through multiple reasoning paths while still having a unique polarity.

We say that an atom $a$ is **polarity-consistent** when exactly one of its two literals is derivable:

$$
G \vdash a
\quad\text{and}\quad
G \nvdash \neg a,
$$

or

$$
G \nvdash a
\quad\text{and}\quad
G \vdash \neg a.
$$

Thus, multiple reasoning paths do not create a conflict as long as they agree on the resulting polarity.

For any source-target pair that reaches only one polarity, the corresponding relation remains endpoint-abstractable.


### Conflict

An atom $a$ is **conflicting** when both its positive and negative literals are derivable:

$$
G \vdash a
\quad\text{and}\quad
G \vdash \neg a.
$$

In this case, the reasoning structure contains a **polarity conflict**.

From the perspective of rules, such a conflict may arise when distinct reasoning paths yield opposite conclusions.

For example,

$$
R_1 : \ell_1 \rightarrow a
$$

and

$$
R_2 : \ell_2 \rightarrow \neg a.
$$

Importantly, the existence of a global conflict does not by itself imply that every source-target relation is non-abstractable.

A conflict may arise because **different premises support different polarities**, while each premise individually determines a unique conclusion.


### Multi-Path Structures and Conflict

A source may participate in multiple outgoing reasoning paths, and a target may be reached through multiple incoming paths.

Path multiplicity is a structural property and does not by itself determine whether a conflict exists.

#### Premise-Separable Conflict

Suppose an atom $a$ is globally conflicting:

$$
G \vdash a
\quad\text{and}\quad
G \vdash \neg a.
$$

The conflict is **premise-separable** when no individual initial premise produces both polarities.

For example,

$$
p \leadsto_G a
$$

and

$$
q \leadsto_G \neg a,
$$

where $p$ and $q$ are different initial premises.

Conditioning on $p$ yields a unique positive conclusion, while conditioning on $q$ yields a unique negative conclusion.

Thus, although the overall reasoning system contains a conflict, each premise-conditioned relation remains endpoint-abstractable.

#### Premise-Internal Conflict

A conflict is **premise-internal** when the same initial premise can reach both polarities of a target atom.

That is, there exists a source literal $s$ such that

$$
s \leadsto_G a
$$

and

$$
s \leadsto_G \neg a.
$$

For example,

$$
s \rightarrow p \rightarrow a
$$

and

$$
s \rightarrow q \rightarrow \neg a.
$$

Because conditioning on $s$ still yields incompatible conclusions, the source-target relation cannot be summarized by a unique endpoint polarity.

It is therefore non-abstractable.

The distinction between the two conflict classes can therefore be summarized as:

| Conflict Class | Global Conflict | Same Premise Reaches<br> Both Polarities | Source-Conditioned <br>Abstraction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Premise-Separable | Yes | No | Possible |
| Premise-Internal | Yes | Yes | Impossible for the conflicting relation |

## 3. Formalization

We now define the symbolic units used throughout this work.


**Atoms.**

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite set of atoms.

An atom $a \in \mathcal{A}$ represents an indivisible propositional symbol without an intrinsic truth value.


**Literals.**

For each atom $a \in \mathcal{A}$, we define two literals corresponding to its polarity:

- $a$: the positive literal

- $\neg a$: the negative literal

We denote the set of all literals by

$$
\mathcal{L}
=
\{a,\neg a \mid a\in\mathcal{A}\}.
$$

For each literal $\ell\in\mathcal L$, let

$$
\operatorname{Atom}(\ell)
$$

denote its underlying atom and

$$
\operatorname{Pol}(\ell)\in\{+,-\}
$$

denote its polarity.


**Rules.**

A rule is a directed implication between two literals.

Formally,

$$
R:\ell_1\rightarrow\ell_2,
$$

where

$$
\ell_1,\ell_2\in\mathcal L.
$$

The set of rules is

$$
\mathcal R\subseteq\mathcal L\times\mathcal L.
$$

A rule states that if $\ell_1$ is supported, then $\ell_2$ becomes supported.


**Initial Valuation.**

Let

$$
v_0:
\mathcal A
\rightarrow
\{\mathsf t,\mathsf f,\mathsf u\}
$$

be a partial initial valuation, where $\mathsf u$ denotes an unspecified atom.

The valuation induces the initial literal set

$$
I_0
=
\{a\mid v_0(a)=\mathsf t\}
\cup
\{\neg a\mid v_0(a)=\mathsf f\}.
$$


**Reasoning Structure.**

A reasoning structure is

$$
G=(\mathcal A,\mathcal R,v_0).
$$

Its associated directed graph has literal set $\mathcal L$ as its nodes and contains an edge

$$
\ell_i\rightarrow\ell_j
$$

for every rule

$$
(\ell_i,\ell_j)\in\mathcal R.
$$


**Reasoning Path.**

A reasoning path is a finite sequence of literals

$$
\tau
=
(\ell_0,\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k)
$$

such that

$$
(\ell_i,\ell_{i+1})\in\mathcal R
$$

for every

$$
0\leq i<k.
$$

We denote the set of valid reasoning paths by

$$
\operatorname{Paths}(G).
$$

A path containing $k$ edges is called a **$k$-hop path**.


**Logical Closure.**

Starting from $I_0$, define

$$
C_0=I_0
$$

and iteratively compute

$$
C_{t+1}
=
C_t
\cup
\{
\ell_2
\mid
(\ell_1,\ell_2)\in\mathcal R,
\ell_1\in C_t
\}.
$$

Inference terminates when

$$
C_{t+1}=C_t.
$$

The resulting set is the least fixed-point closure of $I_0$ under $\mathcal R$:

$$
Cl_{\mathcal R}(I_0).
$$


**Derivability.**

A literal $\ell$ is derivable if

$$
\ell\in Cl_{\mathcal R}(I_0).
$$

We write

$$
G\vdash\ell
$$

to denote derivability.


**Source-Conditioned Reachability.**

For literals $s$ and $\ell$, we write

$$
s\leadsto_G\ell
$$

if there exists a valid reasoning path beginning at $s$ and ending at $\ell$.

This differs from global derivability.

The statement

$$
G\vdash\ell
$$

asks whether $\ell$ can be obtained from any initial premise, whereas

$$
s\leadsto_G\ell
$$

asks whether $\ell$ can be obtained from a particular source literal $s$.


**Source-Conditioned Outcome.**

For a source literal $s$ and target atom $a$, define

$$
O_G(s,a)
=
\{
\ell\in\{a,\neg a\}
\mid
s\leadsto_G\ell
\}.
$$


The main structural properties can then be summarized as follows:

| Name | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Positive | $G\vdash a \;\wedge\; G\nvdash\neg a$ |
| Negative | $G\nvdash a \;\wedge\; G\vdash\neg a$ |
| Unknown | $G\nvdash a \;\wedge\; G\nvdash\neg a$ |
| Conflicting | $G\vdash a \;\wedge\; G\vdash\neg a$ |
| Endpoint-Abstractable | $ \mid O_G(s,a)\mid=1$ |
| Non-Abstractable | $ \mid O_G(s,a)\mid =2$ |
| Premise-Separable Conflict | $G\vdash a \wedge G\vdash\neg a$ and $\forall s\in I_0,\; \mid O_G(s,a)\mid \leq1$ |
| Premise-Internal Conflict | $\exists s\in I_0$ such that $\mid O_G(s,a)\mid =2$ |
